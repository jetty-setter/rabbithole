# RabbitHole — V1 Model Validation Against Non-Mystery Subjects

Companion to [`RABBITHOLE_CONTENT_MODEL.md`](./RABBITHOLE_CONTENT_MODEL.md). The
first five samples skewed to mysteries and unexplained events. This pass drafts
three deliberately **non-mystery** RabbitHoles against the revised V1 structure
and asks one question: does the model still work when nothing is mysterious?

Subjects: an engineering question, a technology/history question, a psychology
question.

1. Why Airplane Windows Are Round
2. How the QWERTY Keyboard Took Over
3. Why We Get Songs Stuck in Our Heads

## Sample 1 — Why Airplane Windows Are Round

> *Body length: ~590 words. Timeline used. "What's contested" used (a correction).*

### TITLE
Why Airplane Windows Are Round

### SUBTITLE
Airliner cabin windows are small ovals with rounded corners rather than squares, a design standard set by the earliest jet airliners.

### HOOK
A pressurized airliner is essentially a balloon. At cruising altitude the cabin
holds roughly eight pounds per square inch more pressure than the thin air
outside, so the aluminium skin stretches slightly on every flight and relaxes on
landing. Do that a few thousand times and any sharp corner in the structure —
including a square window corner — becomes the place a fatigue crack quietly
begins.

### THE SHORT VERSION
Cabin windows have generously rounded corners because a sharp corner concentrates
stress into a small area, and repeated pressurization cycles turn that spot into a
fatigue crack. The lesson was learned expensively. In 1954 two de Havilland
Comets — the first jet airliners — broke apart in flight. Investigators
pressurized a whole fuselage underwater until it failed, and traced the crack to
the corner of a roughly rectangular cabin opening and the rivet holes around it.
Every pressurized aircraft since has used openings with large corner radii and
lower working stresses.

### WHAT WE KNOW
- The de Havilland Comet entered service in 1952 as the first jet airliner; two
  came apart in flight within three months of each other in early 1954.¹
- A UK Court of Inquiry attributed both crashes to fatigue failure of the pressure
  cabin, beginning at a point of concentrated stress.¹
- Investigators repeatedly pressurized an entire Comet fuselage submerged in a
  water tank; it failed after the equivalent of about 3,000 flights.²
- In that test the crack started at the corner of a cabin-roof aperture and nearby
  rivet and bolt holes — not at a passenger window.³
- A sharp interior corner can raise local stress to several times the surrounding
  level; enlarging the corner radius spreads the load.⁴
- The Comet's skin was thin and its rivet holes were punched rather than drilled,
  which left tiny cracks that accelerated fatigue.³
- The redesigned Comet 4 and the Boeing 707, both entering service in 1958, used
  thicker skin, lower working stresses, and oval windows.⁵

### WHAT'S CONTESTED OR STILL OPEN
The popular version — *"the Comet's passenger windows were square, and that's what
failed"* — is a simplification.

**Unsupported as stated.** The Comet 1's passenger windows were more squared-off
than today's but were already given rounded corners. The fatigue crack reproduced
in testing originated at a different, roughly rectangular opening in the cabin
roof and at the fastener holes around it, compounded by punched-hole construction
and skin stresses higher than later standards would allow.

**Established.** The principle the accident taught — that corners, holes and
notches in a cyclically pressurized skin are where cracks start, and that corner
radii, hole finishing and working stress all have to be controlled together — is
not disputed, and it reshaped how every later airframe is designed and inspected.

### TIMELINE
- **1949** — The Comet prototype makes its first flight.
- **May 1952** — BOAC begins Comet 1 service, the first scheduled jet airliner.
- **10 January 1954** — BOAC Flight 781 breaks up near Elba; the fleet is briefly
  grounded, then returns to service.
- **8 April 1954** — South African Airways Flight 201 breaks up near Naples; the
  Comet 1's certificate of airworthiness is withdrawn.
- **Summer 1954** — Farnborough's water-tank test of fuselage G-ALYU fails at a
  cabin-opening corner.
- **February 1955** — The Court of Inquiry publishes its findings on metal fatigue
  and stress concentration.
- **1958** — The redesigned Comet 4 and the Boeing 707 enter service.

### KEEP DIGGING
- **Metal fatigue** — The failure mode at the centre of this story: repeated loads
  far below a material's breaking strength that still crack it over time.
- **Aloha Airlines Flight 243** — In 1988 a 737 lost a large section of its upper
  fuselage to widespread fatigue cracking, forcing today's structural inspection
  rules.
- **Stress concentration** — Why engineers care so much about fillet radii, notch
  shapes and hole placement: these are where almost all parts actually break.
- **The de Havilland Comet** — The aircraft that opened the jet age, lost its head
  start to Boeing after 1954, and never recovered commercially.
- **Cabin pressurization** — How an airliner keeps a breathable cabin at 40,000
  feet, and why that comfort loads the airframe on every single flight.

## Sample 2 — How the QWERTY Keyboard Took Over

> *Body length: ~640 words. Timeline used. "What's contested" used (myth + live
> scholarly dispute) — the centre of gravity for this topic.*

### TITLE
How the QWERTY Keyboard Took Over

### SUBTITLE
QWERTY is the standard Latin-alphabet key layout, arranged in the early 1870s for the first commercial typewriter and never displaced.

### HOOK
The layout on almost every keyboard you have ever used was fixed by the mechanical
limits of a machine from 1873. Christopher Latham Sholes and his collaborators
spent years rearranging keys on an early typewriter whose type bars jammed when
neighbouring letters were struck in quick succession. The arrangement they landed
on outlived the jamming problem, the typewriter itself, and every attempt to
replace it.

### THE SHORT VERSION
QWERTY was shaped by the first typewriters, whose type bars hung in a circular
basket and clashed if two bars near each other were thrown in fast succession.
Separating letters that often occur together reduced the clashing. QWERTY then won
the market rather than a design contest: Remington's early lead, a famous 1888
speed contest that publicised touch typing on it, an 1893 merger that made it the
industry standard, and typing schools that trained millions. Later layouts may be
marginally faster, but the gap is small and retraining costs are not.

### WHAT WE KNOW
- Sholes, Carlos Glidden and Samuel Soule patented an early typewriter in 1868;
  the QWERTY-style arrangement settled around 1873.¹
- E. Remington and Sons began manufacturing the Sholes and Glidden Type-Writer in
  1873; it reached the market in 1874.²
- Early type bars struck a shared printing point and could clash if adjacent bars
  were thrown in quick succession; the layout separated many common letter pairs.²
- In an 1888 Cincinnati contest, court reporter Frank McGurrin touch-typed on
  QWERTY from memory and beat a hunt-and-peck typist, drawing national coverage.²
- In 1893 the main American typewriter manufacturers merged into the Union
  Typewriter Company and adopted QWERTY as a common standard.²
- August Dvorak patented a rival layout in 1936; a 1956 US government study
  concluded that retraining typists onto it was not cost-justified.³
- Controlled studies find only small speed differences between layouts for
  practised typists; practice matters far more than layout.⁴

### WHAT'S CONTESTED OR STILL OPEN
**Debunked:** the claim that QWERTY was designed "to slow typists down." Nothing
in the design record aims at typist speed; the goal was to keep frequently paired
letters from jamming the type bars. A skilled operator on an 1870s machine was
limited by the machine, not the layout.

**Contested — how much of QWERTY's survival is "lock-in."** The economist Paul
David (1985) made QWERTY the textbook example of *path dependence*: an inferior
standard that persists because switching is collectively too expensive.⁵ Stan
Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis (1990) rejected this, arguing the evidence for a
large Dvorak advantage traces mostly to Dvorak's own wartime studies and does not
hold up, so QWERTY was never shown to be meaningfully worse.⁶ Both sides agree the
measurable difference between layouts is small; they disagree on what that implies
about how markets choose standards.

**Open:** the influence of early users. Koichi Yasuoka argues that telegraph
operators transcribing Morse code shaped several key positions.⁷ It is plausible
and partly documented, but not settled.

### TIMELINE
- **1868** — Sholes, Glidden and Soule receive their first typewriter patent.
- **c. 1873** — The QWERTY-style key arrangement takes its lasting form.
- **1874** — Remington ships the Sholes and Glidden Type-Writer.
- **1878** — The Remington No. 2 adds a shift key and lower-case letters and sells
  widely.
- **1888** — McGurrin wins the Cincinnati typing contest.
- **1893** — The Union Typewriter Company standardises on QWERTY.
- **1936** — Dvorak patents his Simplified Keyboard.
- **1956** — A US General Services Administration study finds retraining is not
  worthwhile.

### KEEP DIGGING
- **Path dependence** — The idea that early accidents lock in standards regardless
  of merit; QWERTY is its most-cited example, and also its most-disputed one.
- **The Dvorak keyboard** — The best-known challenger, and a case study in why a
  design that looks better on paper rarely unseats an entrenched one.
- **Containerization** — Another format that beat its rivals partly by chance and
  then became almost impossible to change, reshaping global trade.
- **Touch typing** — The skill that turned the keyboard into infrastructure and
  made switching layouts feel prohibitively costly.
- **Stenotype machines** — A completely different key system that *did* win its
  niche, because the QWERTY incumbent could not serve courtroom transcription
  speeds.

## Sample 3 — Why We Get Songs Stuck in Our Heads

> *Body length: ~600 words. No Timeline (correctly). "What's contested" used
> (unresolved mechanism + an overclaimed finding).*

### TITLE
Why We Get Songs Stuck in Our Heads

### SUBTITLE
An "earworm" — involuntary musical imagery — is a short loop of music that replays in your mind without you choosing it.

### HOOK
Almost everyone has had a song fragment loop in their mind for hours, usually a
chorus whose ending they cannot quite remember. Researchers call it involuntary
musical imagery, and it is one of the most common intrusive thoughts there is. It
also turns out to be a useful window into how memory, attention and the brain's
sound system behave when you are not paying attention to them.

### THE SHORT VERSION
A song sticks when something cues it — you heard it recently or often, or a word
or place is linked to it — and your attention is free enough to wander, so the
tune replays in the brain's sound system on a short loop. The fragment is usually
a 15-to-30-second hook, often a part you don't fully know. Simple, fast, familiar
melodies with one distinctive turn are the most frequent offenders. The mechanism
is not settled, but occupying the same mental machinery — chewing gum, or hearing
the song through to the end — tends to break the loop.

### WHAT WE KNOW
- Surveys find the large majority of people experience earworms at least weekly;
  most are neutral or pleasant, and roughly a third are rated intrusive.¹
- An earworm is typically a looped fragment of about 15–30 seconds, most often the
  chorus or hook.²
- The most commonly reported triggers are recent or repeated exposure to a song,
  word and situation associations, and mind-wandering or low-attention states.³
- Songs named as earworms tend to have faster tempos and common melodic shapes,
  with an unusual interval or repeated figure that sets them apart.⁴
- Silently imagining a familiar song activates the auditory cortex much as
  actually hearing it does.⁵
- In experiments, chewing gum measurably reduces the frequency and vividness of
  earworms.⁶
- Earworms are distinct from musical hallucinations, a clinical phenomenon in
  which music is heard as a real, external sound.⁷

### WHAT'S CONTESTED OR STILL OPEN
**The mechanism is genuinely unresolved.** Three explanations compete and may all
contribute:

- *Memory activation* — a cue reactivates a strongly encoded tune, which then
  lingers. Well supported as a trigger, but it does not by itself explain the
  looping.
- *An attention or working-memory loop* — the tune circulates in the brain's
  "inner voice," which is why articulatory tasks such as chewing gum interrupt it.
  Supported by the gum findings, but partial.
- *The Zeigarnik effect* — unfinished things stay active in memory, so an
  incomplete tune loops seeking closure. There is some support: people who
  mentally play a stuck song to its end often report that it stops.⁸ Still
  contested.

**Unsupported as causal:** a 2015 study linked frequent earworms to differences in
cortical thickness in auditory and emotion-related brain regions.⁹ The sample was
small and the design correlational, so it cannot show that brain structure causes
the tendency.

**Open question:** whether earworms are a by-product of a memory system that keeps
useful sequences primed, or simply noise.

### KEEP DIGGING
- **The Zeigarnik effect** — The finding that unfinished tasks nag at memory more
  than finished ones — one of the leading explanations for why a song loops
  instead of fading.
- **Musical hallucinations** — A sharper version of the same wiring: music the
  brain generates so vividly that people search the house for a radio, usually
  alongside hearing loss.
- **Music and autobiographical memory** — Why a few seconds of an old song can
  return a specific place and year so completely; the same wiring makes a jingle
  stick.
- **Mind-wandering** — Earworms surface in exactly the unfocused mental states
  that also produce daydreams, and share brain networks with them.
- **Advertising jingles** — An industry built on deliberately engineering the
  melodic features that research says make a tune lodge and repeat.

## Cross-sample conclusion

### Does the structure work for ordinary science, engineering, psychology, technology, and history?

**Yes.** All three drafts came in at 590–640 words, read as RabbitHole, and none
needed the mystery scaffolding that broke the first five. The single most
important change from the original model — making **"What's Contested or Still
Open"** and **"Timeline"** genuinely optional — is what fixed it:

- **Engineering (windows):** Timeline and the correction both earned their place;
  "What We Know" carried the body.
- **Technology / history (QWERTY):** the optional section did the heavy lifting (a
  real debunk plus a real scholarly dispute) and was the best part of the piece.
- **Psychology (earworms):** the model flexed correctly — no Timeline, and "What's
  Contested" became "open research questions" without becoming filler.

When there is a real dispute or a real correction, the optional section is among
the strongest parts of the piece. When there is not (earworms Timeline), omitting
it costs nothing. That is the behaviour the model needed.

### Should any field change before engineering freezes the schema?

Four minor adjustments. **None require structural change.**

1. **TITLE — add an accuracy rule.** "Recognizable topic or question" is not
   enough. "Why Airplane Windows Are Round" is catchy but wrong, and the piece
   spends words correcting its own title. Rule: *the title must be accurate; a
   colloquial phrasing is fine only if the body does not have to contradict it.*
   (Here: "Why Airplane Windows Have Rounded Corners," or keep the colloquial
   title and drop the correction from the body.)

2. **WHAT WE KNOW × TIMELINE — no duplicate events.** When both sections are
   present, a dated event appears in one, not both. QWERTY repeated four dates
   across the two. This is the same redundancy that made the first five samples
   feel long; a one-line rule prevents it.

3. **THE SHORT VERSION — raise the ceiling to ~110 words.** Multi-factor answers
   (psychology, biology, anything with "several things contribute") do not fit in
   100 words without losing the honest "what's not known" clause. 60–110 is
   safer than 60–100.

4. **KEEP DIGGING — tighten the "larger context" test.** The weakest links in all
   three drafts (cabin pressurization, mind-wandering) were "larger context"
   ones. Rule: *a "larger context" link must state what the reader now understands
   that makes the context worth reading — not just name the parent topic.* Also
   confirm for V1: **do not display relationship labels** — the drafts omitted
   them and read fine; keep the type as an internal editorial checklist only.

### Is the model broad enough to support RabbitHole as a curiosity engine rather than an unsolved-mystery product?

**Yes** — with one caveat that is about editorial discipline, not the model.

The structure now supports non-mysteries cleanly: a settled engineering story, a
technology history with a live academic argument, and an open psychology question
all fit without strain. The remaining risk is **catalog composition and editorial
habit** — the pull toward framing everything as unsolved — not the fields
themselves.

Recommendation:

- **Freeze the V1 editorial model** after the four adjustments above. Engineering
  can formalise the schema.
- Set an editorial guideline that the catalog stays **majority non-mystery for the
  first ~30 RabbitHoles**, to establish the "curiosity engine" identity before
  mysteries are added at scale.
- Keep the internal relationship-type checklist for connections (Involved · Same
  kind of thing · Bigger picture · What came next · Competing explanation) even
  though the labels are not shown — it is the check that stops glossary links.

**Verdict: the V1 editorial model is ready to freeze after four minor,
non-structural adjustments.**
